## Method and Limits

This is a structured, targeted evidence review completed on **2026-10-09**, not
a preregistered systematic review, meta-analysis, or clinical guideline.

Questions: weekly training dose; strength frequency and progression; combined
strength/cardio order; monthly variation; age-derived heart rate; BMI; and
safety limits. Priority was given to current WHO/ACSM recommendations, official
AHA/CDC guidance, then peer-reviewed reviews and validation studies. Search
terms included `resistance training position stand 2026`, `concurrent training
sequence meta analysis`, `age predicted maximal heart rate error`, `talk test
exercise intensity`, and `adult BMI limitations`.

Journal findings below were checked through abstracts and bibliographic records
in Europe PMC, not a claim of full-text appraisal of every included study.
Official web guidance was checked through retrieved pages or web-search source
extracts. Direct requests to some CDC/AHA pages returned HTTP 403; their relevant
passages were subsequently available through web search. Source availability
does not establish clinical validity for an individual.

## Findings and Product Consequences

| Question | Evidence and limitation | Consequence for Rhino |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Weekly aerobic dose | WHO recommends 150-300 moderate minutes or 75-150 vigorous minutes, or an equivalent mix, for adults [E1]. This is a population health recommendation, not a mandatory starting dose. | Show progress toward a configurable goal, encourage gradual accumulation, and never squeeze the total into insufficient available sessions. |
| Strength frequency | WHO recommends major-muscle strengthening on at least two days [E1]. The 2026 ACSM summary emphasizes training all major muscle groups at least twice weekly [E2]. | Prefer two full-body exposures in a basic plan; explain when availability or a cardio-only choice does not meet this pattern. |
| Sets, load, and equipment | The 2026 ACSM overview covers 137 systematic reviews and over 30,000 adults. Higher loads, complete range of motion, 2-3 sets and at least two weekly sessions can favor strength outcomes; higher weekly volume can favor hypertrophy [E2, E3]. | Begin with tolerable, simple prescriptions. Do not turn optimization findings into compulsory heavy beginner training. Machines, free weights, and bodyweight are legitimate when taught correctly. |
| Training to failure | Failure, equipment type, and complex periodization did not consistently change outcomes across the reviewed evidence [E2, E3]. | Failure is not required. Start below failure and make consistency more important than an elaborate split. |
| Monthly plan refresh | The evidence does not require replacing exercises every month [E2, E3]. | Review and version monthly; retain productive movements, adjust only for a reason, preserve comparability. |
| Strength plus cardio | An updated meta-analysis found no significant overall compromise to maximal strength or hypertrophy; explosive-strength adaptation was more vulnerable, especially within the same session [E4]. | Do not warn that ordinary cardio "kills gains." Separate demanding sessions when the goal and schedule justify it, not by default. |
| Exercise order | A 19-trial review found a modest lower-limb strength advantage for strength before endurance, with no sequence effect on VO2max [E5]. Populations and protocols differ. | For strength priority: brief easy warm-up, strength, optional substantive cardio. For endurance priority: prioritize cardio. Short preparation is not the same as a long endurance block. |
| Warm-up/cool-down | AHA recommends gradual 5-10 minute warm-up and cool-down around aerobic activity [E6]. | Offer adjustable preparation/recovery blocks and movement-specific warm-up sets. The latter are a coaching default, not a finding of this AHA page. |
| Heart-rate estimates | Tanaka proposes `208 - 0.7 * age` [E7]. A validation study reports substantial individual error, approximately 11.4 bpm standard error for that equation in its population [E8]. | Display an estimate, never a measured maximum or safe ceiling. Age cannot determine loads, health status, or tolerance. |
| Intensity checks | AHA offers broad percent-of-maximum zones [E9]; CDC provides a 0-10 effort scale and talk test [E10]. Medications can alter heart rate [E11]. | Explain the scheme and show a subjective alternative. Do not silently mix percent HRmax with heart-rate reserve. |
| BMI | BMI is kg/m squared, not a direct fat measurement; it cannot distinguish muscle from fat [E12]. CDC adult categories apply from age 20. | Show the value and trend with neutral language. Do not prescribe load or fat-loss targets from BMI, or apply adult categories to ages 18-19. |
| Older adults and symptoms | WHO adds balance-focused multicomponent activity for older adults [E1]. Concerning symptoms need clinical attention [E13]. | Do not repurpose the initial general-fitness generator as rehabilitation or a fall-prevention program. Symptoms override workout completion prompts. |

## What Is a Product Heuristic, Not an Established Formula

The initial rep range, repetitions-in-reserve target, session duration, recovery
spacing, substitution rules, progression confirmation, and monthly review
thresholds in [03](03-planning-and-metrics.md) are conservative **product defaults**
requiring technique review and user feedback. Evidence does not identify one
universally optimal strength/cardio ratio or a birthday-to-training-load formula.

The historical 2009 ACSM guidance includes beginner 8-12RM and a 2-10% load
increase after exceeding the target [E14]. It is superseded by the 2026 position
stand and is retained as historical context only. `8-12RM` means a load that
limits repetitions in that range; it is **not** equivalent to doing 8-12 reps
while deliberately leaving repetitions in reserve.

Do not automatically chase ten sets per muscle per week, prescribe >=80% 1RM to
all beginners, or infer absence of risk from a completed questionnaire. Medical
conditions, medication, heat, sleep, experience, and movement tolerance can
matter more than age for an individual session.

## Movement Reference Check - 2026-10-09

- NASM, [Romanian Deadlift Basics](https://blog.nasm.org/romanian-deadlift-basics):
  retrieved the official article. It describes backward hip movement, a neutral
  spine, slightly bent knees and avoiding excessive knee bending that turns the
  hinge into a squat. Depth depends on maintaining position and hamstring
  tolerance, not reproducing the model's endpoint.
- NASM, [Dumbbell Romanian Deadlift demonstration](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=aa57T45iFSE):
  checked YouTube oEmbed attribution and the watch-page metadata/description.
  The publisher is National Academy of Sports Medicine; metadata reports
  `playableInEmbed=true`. The description calls for dumbbells in front of the
  thighs, close to the shins, with a flat back. Its example knee angle and depth
  are not universal prescriptions. The app links the original and loads the
  privacy-enhanced player only after an explicit action; availability can change.
  A live browser check from the Caddy development origin loaded and played the
  embedded video (video readyState 4, currentTime advanced, no media error).
  Opening the bare embed URL without a referring page returned player error 153;
  that is not the application flow. Keep a valid origin referrer and source link.
- [ACE hip hinge](https://www.acefitness.org/resources/everyone/exercise-library/33/hip-hinge/)
  was identified by the independent review, but direct retrieval returned 403.
  It is a further-reading link, not additional directly verified evidence here.

The authored rig now rotates pelvis and torso together rather than bending one
lumbar joint. Technical checks cover planted feet, stable RDL knee flexion, long
arms, a leg-adjacent wrist path, fixed pulldown hand spacing and modest lateral
raise elbow flexion. These are **regression checks, not clinical or professional
movement approval**. The 11 clips retain `draft` / `instructionReady=false`.

## Review Policy

Recheck the source and algorithm documents when a cited guideline changes or
when adding a new population, intensity scheme, or exercise modality. A monthly
user plan review is not a monthly clinical-evidence update. Store the algorithm
version with generated plans so historical recommendations remain explainable.
